I am a History Ph.D. in the field of early medieval China. My specialization is in Digital Humanities, integrating digital methodology with traditional historical scholarship. I am also interested in the complex interaction between Chinese civilization and steppe peoples, which perennially combined into "conquest dynasties." 

My research concerns a period of disunity and change in early medieval China (~300-618 CE), a period
which featured first acknowledged non-Chinese dynasties [^1] established north of the Yangzi River.
Several key questions drive my research: How did a central regime, ruled by people once considered
“barbarians” by their Chinese subjects, gain these elite families’ allegiance and successfully appropriate
their subjects’ culture to reorient the center of Chinese political and social power to the north? What were
the standards by which families were deemed “Chinese” or “non-Chinese,” “elite” and “common,” over
the course of China’s early medieval period?
I use digital methodologies to conduct my research. I created a database of over nine thousand
individuals using funerary epitaphs and biographies from official historical records. With the use of
networking, mapping, and statistical programs to analyze this data, I have found two overarching
theories on the sociopolitical development of China’s Northern Dynasties. The first is that the successive
northern dynasties – all of which were ruled by northern and non-Chinese people – adhered to a distinct
marriage system which remained consistent up to the early seventh century. This marital system
influenced imperial policies geared towards the unification of the various northern people to create a
unified empire. The second theory is that subjects of these northern dynasties responded to these
policies through their own marital strategies, which in turn created distinct groups of elite northern
families.

[^1] By non-Chinese, I mean both culturally and by descent. Northern rulers who claimed descent from a noble and well-established Chinese lineage either
fabricated their lineage wholesale or had lived for generations in northern borderlands to the point where their families were indistinguishable from their
steppe compatriots.
